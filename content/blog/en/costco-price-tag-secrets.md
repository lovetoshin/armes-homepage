Among Costco regulars, there's a saying: "You can tell if something's on sale just by reading the price tag." It sounds like folklore at first, but the **last digits of a Costco price and the asterisk (\*) really do carry consistent signals.** People who know this code and people who don't experience the same store very differently. One person spots an item on sale that might disappear soon. The other just walks past it.

The signals are easy to memorize too. Just two things: **the last digits and the asterisk.** This post covers what they mean, how to read combinations of them, and where price tags alone fall short.

## What the Last Digits Mean

The most famous rule on Costco price tags is the **last digit.**

- **Ends in .99:** Usually the regular price. Think of this as the standard selling price.
- **Ends in .97:** Often a **marked-down (on-sale) item.** The store has lowered the price to move inventory.
- **Ends in .00 or .88:** May indicate a special clearance price handled by the store — sometimes read as an additional markdown signal.

In other words, **.97 is worth a second look.**

## What the Asterisk (*) Means

A **small asterisk (\*) in the top right corner** of the price tag is an important signal. It typically means "**this item may not be restocked.**" It appears on products being discontinued or on seasonal items that won't come back. If a favorite item of yours suddenly has an asterisk, it might not be there next time — so grab it while you can, if you need it.

## Reading Price Tag Combinations

| Combination | Interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| **.97 + Asterisk** | On sale + being discontinued → "Now or never" |
| **.97 only** | On sale → Worth stocking up if you use it regularly |
| **.99 + Asterisk** | Regular price but being discontinued → Secure it if needed |
| **.99 only** | Regular price → No urgency |

Know these combinations and you can make a decision in seconds on the floor.

## Where Price Tags Alone Fall Short

A price tag only shows you what's happening **right now.** To know whether a price is actually lower than usual, you need to see the **price history.** The same .97 carries very different value depending on whether it came down from an expensive baseline or was already cheap. ## Things to Keep in Mind

- **These are signals, not absolute rules.** There can be exceptions by store and timing — always check the accompanying signage.
- **Don't let the asterisk pressure you.** "It's going away soon" shouldn't push you into buying something unnecessary.
- **Verify .97 with unit price.** Even on sale, if the unit price is higher than your local store, it's not a deal.

## Conclusion

Costco price tags are more than numbers — they're **a signal board for sales and discontinuations.** **.97 means on sale, asterisk means being discontinued** — keep just these two in mind and you'll shop much smarter in the store. ## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

**Q. Does a price ending in .97 always mean it's on sale?**
It generally signals a markdown. However, there can be exceptions by store and timing, so always check the label alongside.

**Q. What does .99 mean?**
Usually the regular price. Think of it as the standard selling price — there's no urgency to buy.

**Q. Where does the asterisk (*) appear?**
In the small top-right corner of the price tag. It signals the item may not be restocked and could be discontinued.

**Q. Does an asterisk mean I have to buy it right now?**
If you need the item, it's safer to grab it while you see it. But don't let "it's going away" push you into buying something you don't actually need.

**Q. What does .97 + asterisk mean?**
On sale and being discontinued — the strongest buy signal: "Now or never."

**Q. What does .00 or .88 mean?**
It may indicate a special clearance price. Check the surrounding signage for more context.

**Q. Can I just buy based on the discount markings?**
Price tags only show "right now." To know if it's truly cheaper than usual, you also need to check price history and unit price.

**Q. Do these signals work the same at all Costco locations?**
It's a general pattern, not an absolute rule. There may be variations by location, country, and time period.
