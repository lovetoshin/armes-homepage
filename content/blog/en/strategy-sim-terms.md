Strategy simulation games have incredible depth — but that depth comes with a steep wall of unfamiliar terminology right at the start. "Develop your domestic affairs." "Your supply line is cut." "Morale has dropped." If you don't know what these mean, you're left staring at a screen full of buttons with no idea what to press, stumbling through your first campaign. In fact, most new players who quit the genre don't leave because it's boring — they leave because **they never understood the terminology**.

This guide is designed for players opening a strategy sim for the first time. It breaks down the most common terms into three categories — domestic management, diplomacy, and combat — and explains how each one connects to what you actually see on screen. Get through this, and your first playthrough will actually make sense.

## Domestic Management Terms

### Domestic Development
Everything involved in building up your nation's internal foundation — growing agriculture, commerce, and population. It might seem less exciting than battle, but **weak domestic development means your war effort collapses from within**. It's the first thing a new player should invest in.

### Resources / Provisions (Grain Supply)
The material needed to run your nation and keep your armies in the field. **Provisions — your grain supply — are the lifeblood of any campaign**. When they run dry, even the strongest army crumbles. "An army marches on its stomach" is the iron rule of this genre.

### Development / Investment
Actions that raise a region's agricultural or commercial output. Choosing where to invest first determines how quickly your faction grows. Since resources are always limited, **prioritization is everything**.

## Diplomacy Terms

### Alliance
An agreement with another faction not to attack each other. It buys you time against stronger opponents and is the most basic tool for **avoiding a two-front war**.

### Relations (Diplomatic Standing)
A numerical score representing how friendly two factions are with each other. High relations make alliances and trade easier; low relations increase the risk of conflict. You can build relations gradually through gifts and trade.

## Combat Terms

### Supply Line
The route used to transport resources to your forces at the front. **Cut the supply line, and your army starves and weakens.** This is why severing an enemy's supply line is one of the most powerful tactical moves in the genre.

### Morale
Your unit's fighting spirit and will to engage. High morale makes your forces fight harder; low morale makes them crumble easily. Winning streaks and stable supplies raise morale; defeats, encirclements, and supply disruptions lower it.

### Formation / Terrain
**Formation** refers to how your troops are deployed on the battlefield. **Terrain** is the effect that mountains, rivers, and the physical landscape have on combat. The same forces can produce very different outcomes depending on both. Avoid fighting uphill battles on unfavorable ground.

## Your First Playthrough — A Practical Example

Now that you know the terms, here's how a solid first campaign actually unfolds:

```text
1. Start with domestic development: build up grain supply and treasury
   (Going to war first drains resources immediately ❌)
2. Secure diplomacy: form an alliance with a strong neighbor → avoid a two-front war
3. Build your army: only as large as your grain supply can support
4. Decisive battle: engage on favorable terrain, all-in at the right moment
5. Consolidate: after capturing territory, manage public sentiment and morale
```

## Common Beginner Mistakes — Before and After

| Mistake | Result | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Declare war before building up domestically | Resources dry up, self-destruction | Domestic development first |
| Two-front war | Forces spread thin, defeats mount | Use an alliance to neutralize one flank |
| Campaign without securing supply lines | Army starves, grows weak | Secure supply lines before advancing |
| Frontal assault on unfavorable terrain | Heavy losses | Lure the enemy to favorable ground |

## What Matters More Than the Vocabulary

You don't need to memorize all of this before you play. The terms will become natural as you go. What really matters is grasping **the big picture: build strength through domestic development, buy time through diplomacy, and be decisive in battle**.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

**Q. Is a strategy simulation game hard to get into?**
Once you know the terms and the basic flow, no. Most difficulty comes from not understanding the vocabulary. Grasp the three pillars — domestic, diplomacy, combat — and you're ready to start.

**Q. What's the very first thing I should do?**
Domestic development — build up agriculture and commerce. Picking a fight before you have resources is a fast road to collapse.

**Q. Why are provisions / grain supply so important?**
It's what keeps your army in the field. When provisions run out, even elite forces starve and lose effectiveness. Always secure your grain supply before a campaign.

**Q. When should I form an alliance?**
When you're surrounded by strong factions and risk a two-front war. Locking one flank with an alliance lets you focus your forces on a single front.

**Q. How do I raise morale?**
Win battles, maintain stable provisions, and keep your army fed. Conversely, defeats, encirclements, and supply disruptions all tank morale.

**Q. What does "cut the supply line" actually mean in practice?**
It means blocking the route your enemy uses to ferry supplies to their army. Starved of resources, their forces weaken — making it a powerful strategy to win without direct confrontation.

**Q. Does terrain actually change outcomes significantly?**
Yes, significantly. Mountains, rivers, and chokepoints can reverse the odds even with equal or inferior forces. Never commit to a frontal assault on bad terrain.

**Q. Do I need to know Korean history to enjoy a Later Three Kingdoms game?**
Not at all. Understanding the strategy mechanics is more than enough to start. The characters — Gyeon Hwon, Gung Ye, Wang Geon — and their stories will naturally become familiar as you play.
