When you walk into Costco, you get this feeling that everything here is a deal — and your cart fills up fast. Then you see the total at checkout and think, "Hm, that added up more than I expected." Here's the truth: **not everything at Costco is cheap.** Because it's in bulk, the total price looks big, but when you compare by unit price, some items are similar to — or even more expensive than — your local grocery store.

To actually get value at Costco, you need to drop the belief that "everything here is cheap" and replace it with **a clear standard for what's genuinely good value.** This post covers how to compare by unit price, which categories deliver real value versus which to approach with caution, and a checklist to run before you put anything in your cart.

## The Real Standard Is Unit Price

The biggest trap at Costco is bulk size. "One container for 10,000 won" looks cheap — but you might be able to get the same amount for less by buying smaller portions elsewhere. That's why comparisons should always be done in terms of **price per 100g, per item, or per liter**. Unit pricing is often printed small on the price tag, so get in the habit of looking for that number.

## Good Value vs. Approach With Caution

| Good value | Approach with caution |
| --- | --- |
| Toilet paper, detergent, hygiene products (you'll use it all) | Fresh food in bulk (spoilage = money lost) |
| Nuts, canned goods, coffee (long shelf life) | Clothing, electronics, seasonal items (impulse buys) |
| Zip-lock bags, parchment paper (consumables) | Rarely-used spices in huge quantities (often thrown away) |
| Frozen foods you eat regularly | Oversized portions for single-person households |

### Where You Get Real Value
- **Long-lasting household staples:** Toilet paper, detergent, hygiene products. You'll use it all eventually, so the unit price advantage holds.
- **Shelf-stable foods:** Items like nuts, canned goods, and coffee with long expiration dates.
- **Frequently-used consumables:** Things like zip-lock bags and parchment paper — the kind of thing you always need more of.

### Approach With Caution
- **Fresh food in bulk:** If it spoils before you use it, the low unit price doesn't matter — you're still losing money. Consider your household size.
- **Impulse-buy zones:** Clothing, electronics, and seasonal displays make it easy to grab things because they're there, not because you need them.
- **Things you only use occasionally:** A giant container of a spice you rarely use will almost certainly end up in the trash.

## A 4-Question Checklist Before You Buy

| Question | Pass criteria |
| --- | --- |
| Have I compared by unit price? | Price per unit, not total cost |
| Will I actually use all of this? | Shelf life × household size |
| Do I have space to store it? | Bulk takes up a lot of room |
| Do I genuinely need this? | Not just "grabbing it because it's there" |

If an item passes all four, it's worth buying.

## Things to Keep in Mind

- **Beware the "everything's a deal" myth.** Without comparing unit prices, expensive items can still feel cheap.
- **Expiration date = real cost.** If you throw it out unused, no unit price is low enough to make it a saving.
- **Don't be swayed by samples.** Tasting something doesn't obligate you to buy it.

## Conclusion

The key to getting genuine value at Costco is **comparing by unit price and only buying what you'll actually use.** Don't assume bulk means cheap — factor in unit price and actual usage together. Apply that standard consistently, and Costco becomes a genuinely smart place to shop.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

**Q. Isn't everything at Costco cheap?**
Not quite. Because items come in bulk, the total price looks high and the per-item price can seem low — but when compared by unit price, some items are similar to or more expensive than your local store.

**Q. How do I compare by unit price?**
Convert to price per 100g, per item, or per liter. Unit pricing is often printed small on the Costco price tag.

**Q. Is Costco worth it for a single-person household?**
For household staples and shelf-stable foods, yes. But fresh food in bulk is easy to waste. Check the quantity and whether you have storage space first.

**Q. Which items are especially good value?**
Household staples like toilet paper and detergent, shelf-stable foods like nuts and coffee, and consumables like zip-lock bags tend to have strong unit price competitiveness.

**Q. Should I avoid fresh foods entirely?**
Not if you can actually use it all. But if you don't account for your household size and expiration date, the cost of waste often exceeds the savings.

**Q. How do I reduce impulse buying?**
Make a list before you go, and ask yourself "Do I actually need this?" one extra time in clothing, electronics, and seasonal sections.

**Q. Should I also look at price tag signals?**
Yes. Combining .97 (discount) and asterisk (*) (being discontinued) with unit price comparison leads to smarter purchases.

**Q. Is Costco cheaper than online?**
It depends on the item. Comparing unit prices against online lowest prices before deciding is the safest habit.
